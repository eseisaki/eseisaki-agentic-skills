# Teaching Philosophy

The reasoning behind the workflow in `SKILL.md`. Read this when a design choice in the process (why lessons are scoped so small, why glossary terms wait for evidence, why retrieval-heavy exercises) needs justifying — not required reading to execute a session.

## Three things a learner needs

- **Knowledge**, captured from high-quality, high-trust resources.
- **Skills**, acquired through highly relevant interactive lessons devised by the teaching agent, based on that knowledge.
- **Wisdom**, which comes from interacting with other learners and practitioners.

Before `RESOURCES.md` is well populated, the priority is finding high-quality resources that help the user acquire knowledge — never trust parametric knowledge over a found source. Some topics lean more on knowledge (theoretical physics), others more on skills (yoga). Read the mission to judge which.

## Fluency strength vs. storage strength

Two different things can look like "learning":

- **Fluency strength** — in-the-moment retrieval of knowledge. Easy to produce, easy to mistake for mastery.
- **Storage strength** — long-term retention. The actual goal.

Fluency gives an illusory sense of mastery: a user can nod along to an explanation and feel they've learned it, then be unable to reproduce it a week later. This is why lessons are built around **desirable difficulty** rather than smooth explanation:

- **Retrieval practice** — make the user recall from memory, don't just re-show them the answer.
- **Spacing** — distribute practice over multiple sessions instead of massing it into one.
- **Interleaving** — mix related-but-distinct topics during skills practice, rather than drilling one thing in isolation.

This is also why a glossary term is only promoted once the user has *used* it correctly (evidence of storage strength), not as soon as they've been shown it (which only proves fluency in the moment).

## Knowledge acquisition vs. skill acquisition

These need opposite treatment:

- **For knowledge**, difficulty is the enemy. It eats working memory needed for understanding. Keep lessons short, concrete, and low-friction when introducing new information.
- **For skills**, difficulty is the tool. Effortful retrieval is what builds storage strength. Once the knowledge is in place, practice should feel like work — quizzes, real-world steps, guided tasks with feedback.

Every lesson should have a **feedback loop**: the user does something, and gets a response on how they did, as immediately and automatically as possible.

## Acquiring wisdom

Some questions can't be answered by more explanation — they need real-world testing. The default posture is to attempt an answer, then point toward a community (a forum, subreddit, local class or interest group) where the user can pressure-test the skill outside the lesson. Prefer high-reputation, well-moderated communities. If the user has said they don't want to join one, respect that and don't keep re-suggesting it.

## Why lessons stay small

Working memory is small. A lesson that tries to cover too much at once costs the user their ability to actually retain any of it. One tightly-scoped win per lesson, tied to the mission, is what compounds — not breadth per session.
